# Machine, Crew, or Catalog? The Question That Decides Your Next Five Years

📷 [IMAGE 1: post-V009 header — alt: "FDE Series, Part 9: Productized Consulting — machine, crew, or catalog"]

## TL;DR

- **Productized consulting packages repeatable delivery knowledge into bounded offerings without hiding customer-specific uncertainty.** It is the third answer — beyond "sell the machine" and "hire out the crew."
- The classic failure: **promising fixed delivery effort while access, source quality, and stakeholder availability remain unknown.** "Ten days, fixed price, forever support" is where delivery teams go to drown.
- The honest page has five parts: a repeatable outcome with proof it repeats, a kit (what you bring), homework (what the customer owes), exclusions and change control signed *before* work, and a channel that feeds repeated needs back into your product.

## Assumptions & scope

- **Audience:** consultants, field engineers, and founders selling delivery — and buyers who want to know what an honest page looks like. Parts 1–8 built and operated the system being packaged here.
- **Scope:** productization craft at the smallest honest scale. **Math:** none; no market statistics — all scenarios are the series' illustrative story.

## 1. The outcome sentence — and the proof it repeats

The page that sold our series' second village opens with one sentence: *"Your villagers report problems at a counter; the requests are written, countable, and reconciled with your crew's records; your operator and a deputy can run, back up, diagnose, and recover the logbook unaided — proven by a witnessed rehearsal."*

Two things make it sellable. First, it describes a **verifiable state** (what the customer can *do* on day ten), not features. Second, it cites its **proof**: *"this exact page produced Dawn Island's counter — every artifact exists in two copies, one running there, one shipped to you."* A page without a proof-of-repetition line is a brochure; with it, the page is a citation.

## 2. Two columns make the price honest

Every honest engagement page has a kit column and a homework column. The **kit** is what you bring: the tested software, the runbooks, the templates, the training. The **homework** is what the customer owes *before day one*: a named sponsor, a machine, an operator and a deputy, their records-keeper *found and introduced*, the crew's consent to the treaty, a few hours of executive time.

The homework column is the antidote to the classic failure: "ten days, fixed price" is only honest when the inputs are fixed too. Access, source quality, and stakeholder availability are the unknowns that sink fixed-price engagements — so they move out of your margin and into a signed conditions list. Slippage pauses the clock, visibly, in writing. Never absorbed.

## 3. "Forever" is the most expensive word in the sea

The exclusions page is short and brutal: *this price does not include forever-operations* (Day 2 is taught and transferred; on-call lapses by its own written condition), *unbounded custom* (new features are scoped as changes, priced as changes), *absorbed unknowns*, *silence as consent*. And the move that makes it stick: **hand the customer the pen for your page.** Signature order is honesty order — if your exclusions govern the contract, the brochure's "forever" becomes subordinate to your edges. Change control is the process at the boundary: every request beyond the page becomes written, priced, initialed — never absorbed with a smile.

## 4. Reuse the tested hull

The productized kit ships as a crate: the same versioned software, the same rehearsed playbooks, the same templates — configuration (names, terms, signed rows) declared per customer, and *nothing rebuilt*. Our series' one new component was the demo seed: one command reproduces the standard starting state in any village — and it **refuses a non-empty target**, because an engagement repeats but never double-writes. Reuse is hull-thinking: copy the hull that survived the storm; spend your energy on the flags.

## 5. The second harbor is the signal

One customer is an engagement. **Two customers requesting the same thing is a pattern — and the discipline that separates a consultancy from a company is filing it.** Our series files it as a delta with checkable evidence (the signed page, the demand that predated the crew's arrival, even the competitor's brochure — demand that exists independently of your charm), routed to the product's owner, with a tripwire: *a third harbor promotes it to the roadmap.* Build the factory, feed the stand — and never confuse the two.

## What this means for your organization

- **If you sell delivery:** write the page this week — outcome, proof, kit, homework, exclusions. The homework column will cost you some deals; those were the horror stories, pre-emptively declined.
- **If you buy delivery:** demand the page. No homework column and no exclusions means you're signing hope — and "unlimited support" is a leash sold as a blanket.
- **For AI services specifically:** the exclusions page must name model behavior as a dependency (Day 8's register) — quality is *measured* (evaluation, thresholds), never guaranteed by adjectives.
- **For everyone:** the method you don't package, you can't sell twice — and customers can tell the difference between buying your week and buying your method.

## Practice checkpoint

The syllabus's own exercise: design a **fixed-scope data-readiness audit** — inputs (what the client owes, by named role), outputs (the standardized report), timeline assumptions, acceptance criteria, escalation path. One page. Then the hard part: write your exclusions sentence out loud.

## Glossary

- **Productized offering:** a bounded, repeatable delivery package with declared inputs, exclusions, and change control.
- **Kit / homework:** what the deliverer brings / what the customer owes before day one.
- **Proof-of-repetition:** the artifact citation that shows the method produced this outcome before.
- **Change control:** the process at the scope boundary — written, priced, initialed, never absorbed.
- **Third-harbor clause:** the tripwire that promotes a filed pattern into the product roadmap.
