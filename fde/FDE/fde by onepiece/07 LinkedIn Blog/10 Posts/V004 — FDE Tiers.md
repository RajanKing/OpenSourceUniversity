# FDE Tiers: The Title Is Fleet-Local; the Evidence Travels

📷 [IMAGE 1: post-V004 header — alt: "FDE Series, Part 4: FDE Tiers — stripes are earned per fleet, not per sea"]

## TL;DR

- "FDE tiers" are **employer-specific descriptions of technical scope, customer ownership, and product influence — not a universal credential ladder.** Two companies' "Tier 3" can differ in exactly the decisions that matter.
- Five skills make that usable: locating roles on the implementation↔research spectrum, reading autonomy as decision rights, separating seniority from domain specialization, decoding hiring rubrics, and treating compensation reports as dated, local signals.
- The classic failure: **preparing for a generic FDE title when the actual role requires a different technical profile.**

## Assumptions & scope

- **Audience:** candidates navigating FDE-family titles, and hiring panels writing rubrics for them. Parts 1–3 define the role, the transition, and responsibility design.
- **Scope:** tier and role literacy. **Math:** none. All compensation discussion is framework-only — we cite no unqualified numbers.

## 1. The spectrum: workshop ↔ library

Two FDEs with the same title can have opposite Tuesdays. The implementation-heavy FDE is judged on **delivered, running systems** (integrations weekly, uptime, adoption). The research-adjacent FDE is judged on **defensible knowledge** (evaluation methodology, analyses, decisions improved). Four probes locate any posting:

1. What fraction of the week produces shipped code vs. analysis?
2. Is "done" a deployment or a defensible conclusion?
3. Does failure look like an outage or a wrong recommendation?
4. Who consumes your output — the customer's workflow, or the company's model/eval team?

Most postings sit between the poles — locate them; don't assume the title did it for you.

## 2. Autonomy is decision rights, not vibes

Ask of any seat: **what may this person decide alone, and what must they escalate?** A typical FDE ladder compresses to watch levels: T1 executes defined tasks and escalates scope · T2 owns a workstream's technical approach · T3 owns the engagement and sets the approach · T4 owns a portfolio and sets strategy.

The diagnostic that saves careers: when offered a "senior" title, ask for the **escalation matrix** — "which decisions still require sign-off in this seat?" If the answer is "everything except how I write the code," the title is compensation theater over a junior decision set. Titles *narrate* autonomy; evidence *earns* it.

## 3. Two rulers, one career

Seniority and specialization are different rulers — and careers die on the assumption that the vertical one moves you along the horizontal one.

- **Seniority** = cumulative general trust: autonomy, communication, delivery discipline. Transfers at full value the day you switch.
- **Domain specialization** = verified waters sailed: industries, system types, compliance regimes, integration patterns. Compounds only with time-in-waters; cannot be cranked.

The practical map: growing seniority in your home domain is the fast lane; adjacent domains cost a context tax (your seniority reads as junior until you re-earn local context); foreign domains are near-resets. Seniority gets you the interview; the domain chart passes it — a "senior FDE, healthcare" seat gates on both.

## 4. Read the race: hiring rubrics as scoring functions

A job description is the shadow of a rubric — the scoring function the panel will actually apply. Reconstruct it in five steps: what does the panel **verify** (repos, references, live scenarios)? What are the visible **weights**? What **recently burned them** (the oddly specific must-have is a fossil of an incident)? What does a great **first 90 days** mean there? And which ruler do they hold — **judgment artifacts or survival counts?**

Then answer the fossil row directly. A "20% — incident behavior" rubric line means someone once froze during an outage; arrive with one STAR-format incident story, told within your actual decision rights. Candidates who fail loops are usually strong — along a ruler the panel wasn't holding.

## 5. Compensation reports are fish-market boards

"The highest-paid AI role" headlines are yesterday's prices at one stall, published as the law of the sea. Before believing any comp number, five questions: **when** was it collected (AI-role data ages in months)? **Where**? **Who's in the sample** (self-reports inflate; posted ranges anchor high)? **What's the split** (base/equity/bonus; junior/senior — an unsplit average is noise)? **What's the trend**, and what drives it?

Use reports for orders of magnitude, structural demand signals, and negotiation ranges from *your own market and date* — never as point-value promises. If the plan doesn't survive the number being halved, the gazette was the reason, and the reason was a fossil fish.

## What this means for your organization

- **Hiring panels:** publish rubric-shaped JDs — what's verified, what's weighted, what the first 90 days mean. In an immature role family, this is your biggest quality lever; vibe-based reqs are why the same title churns.
- **Managers, calibrating:** grade per-column (delivery evidence, autonomy evidence, portfolio evidence), not per-title — two "Tier 2s" with different columns are different jobs.
- **Candidates:** before any application, write one page — spectrum placement, watch level, two-axis position, reconstructed rubric, dated comp range. That page beats a hundred gazettes.
- **For founders:** wide compensation variance across sources for the same title signals an immature, poorly standardized market — upside for evidence-rich hires, repricing risk for title-chasers. Your rubric *is* your protection.

## Practice checkpoint

The syllabus's own exercise: take **three real FDE postings** and compare them across six dimensions — coding depth, cloud access, travel, model work, enterprise integration, ownership — plus spectrum placement and the reconstructed rubric for each. End with a verdict per posting: *fit / stretch / wrong race*, and name the deciding dimension.

## Glossary

- **Implementation-heavy / research-adjacent:** judged on shipped systems vs. defensible knowledge.
- **Decision rights / watch level:** the classes of decisions a seat may conclude alone vs. must escalate.
- **Seniority vs. domain specialization:** cumulative general trust vs. verified waters sailed — two axes, not one ladder.
- **Rubric / fossil row:** the panel's scoring function / the hyper-specific requirement left by a past incident.
- **Comp check:** the five questions (date, geography, sample, split, trend) that turn a salary number into information.
