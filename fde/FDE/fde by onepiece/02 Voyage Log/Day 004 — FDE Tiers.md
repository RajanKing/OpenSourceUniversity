### 🏝️ Location 1 — Compare implementation-heavy and research-adjacent roles

#### 🎭 Journal Scene

The Grand Line inspector, warming to the crew now, lays out three postings from his folder — real ones, he insists, from three different ships.

The first wants an FDE who "ships integrations weekly: SQL, APIs, dashboards, customer environments." The second wants one who "designs evaluation methodology for deployed AI systems; publishes internally." The third: "own the customer outcome; choose the models; build what's missing."

Franky reads the first and sighs happily. Robin reads the second with something dangerously close to a smile. Luffy reads the third and grins. "These are three different jobs with the same title?!"

"Three points on one spectrum," Jinbe says. "Learn to see the axis before you read the salary."

#### 📖 Feynman Notes

**🍖 ELI5 — Luffy's version:**

"Some builders live in the **workshop** — sawdust, hammers, shipping real things every week! Some live in the **library** — reading, testing ideas, finding out what's TRUE even if nothing ships for months! And some lucky ones get a workbench *inside* the library. FDE titles can mean workshop-FDE, library-FDE, or workbench-FDE — same hat, totally different day! Don't ask 'is it an FDE job?' Ask '**how much sawdust, how much paper?**'"

> **⚠️ Where the analogy breaks:** a workshop trades speed for depth permanently; roles mix and *shift* — an implementation-heavy FDE at a startup does evaluation research in month four when the customer demands proof. The exact technical distinction: **implementation-heavy roles are judged on delivered, running systems (latency, uptime, adoption); research-adjacent roles are judged on defensible knowledge (methodology, evals, internal publications, decisions improved).** The four probes that locate any posting: (1) What fraction of the week produces shipped code vs. analysis? (2) Is "done" defined by deployment or by a defensible conclusion? (3) Does failure look like an outage or a wrong recommendation? (4) Which internal team consumes your output — the customer's workflow, or the company's model/eval team?

**⚔️ STANDARD — Engineer's version:**

| Probe | Implementation-heavy leans… | Research-adjacent leans… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly output | Integrations, deploys, fixes | Eval designs, analyses, write-ups |
| Definition of done | Running in the customer's environment | A defensible answer, peer-reviewed internally |
| Failure shape | Outage, missed go-live | Wrong methodology, misled product decision |
| Consumers | The customer's workflow | Model/eval/product teams |
| Skill investment | Breadth + speed + delivery craft | Depth + statistics + experimental rigor |

Most FDE postings sit *between* the poles — the syllabus's point is that you must locate them, not assume the title did it for you.

**Skypiea sidebar ☠️:** the spectrum is also a *contract risk*. Implementation-heavy roles evaluate on delivery metrics — accept only if you control the variables (access, environment, decision latency) those metrics depend on. Research-adjacent roles evaluate on judgment — accept only if someone senior will actually read the work. The failure profiles are asymmetric: the first burns you out on impossible deadlines; the second starves you of impact. Interview both directions: "how was the last person in this seat evaluated?"

#### 🔬 Reverse-Engineering Notes

Dissect the third posting — "own the customer outcome; choose the models; build what's missing":

1. **"Own the outcome"** — accountability language (Day 3's grid): implementation-side, senior autonomy signals.
2. **"Choose the models"** — research-adjacent authority: model selection is an eval-informed decision, so the role includes eval design whether the posting says so or not.
3. **"Build what's missing"** — the implementation hammer: whatever the research side concludes, you ship it.
4. **The composite:** a *workbench-in-the-library* role — the posting is one sentence wearing three hats. Removement test: delete each clause and the job changes shape — which is how you know all three are load-bearing, and why "generic FDE prep" (the failure mode) fails all three at once.

#### 🏗️ Architecture, Trade-offs & Failure Modes

- **Simplest workable approach:** score any posting 1–5 on the four probes; the pattern names itself.
- **Alternative:** trust the title — this is precisely the syllabus's Common Failure Mode wearing a lifejacket: *preparing for a generic FDE title when the actual role requires a different technical profile* — prepping integration skills for an eval-design seat, or statistics for a shipping seat.
- **Failure modes:** *title anchoring* (symptom: surprise in week two — fix: probe before accepting); *spectrum envy* (training for the pole you're not on because it sounds more prestigious — fix: match prep to the *majority* probe score, not the flattery).

#### ⚓ The Crew Debate — "Which pole should the crew train for?"

- **ZORO:** "Both. Grind the spectrum." *(The grind is real, first mate, but the *order* matters — pick a lane, cross-train later.)*
- **ROBIN:** "Research-adjacent. Evidence and evaluation decide who is trusted." *(Says the archaeologist. Who evaluates the evaluators?)*
- **JINBE (the calm):** "Train for the seat you can *evidence* today, and let the adjacent pole be your second sword. The posting tells you which hand the fleet needs."

**Resolution:** train toward the pole your current artifacts already support (Day 2's audit), acquire the other deliberately with a dated plan. **Trade-off that remains:** a two-pole profile takes longer to mint than a one-pole one — priced in tangerines, not wished away.

### 🏝️ Location 2 — Identify expected autonomy

#### 🎭 Journal Scene

The East Blue inspector, flipping her own grade sheet, reads a row aloud: "Autonomy: Tier 1 — executes defined tasks; escalates scope. Tier 2 — owns an engagement workstream. Tier 3 — owns the engagement; sets approach. Tier 4 — owns the portfolio; sets strategy."

Nami copies the ladder in one motion. "That's not seniority fluff — that's a *decision-rights table*. Watch levels on a ship: who may alter course alone, who must wake the captain, who *is* the captain."

Zoro, arms crossed: "So at Tier 4 nobody wakes you."

"At Tier 4," Jinbe says, "you are woken only by consequences. It is less restful than it sounds."

#### 📖 Feynman Notes

**🍖 ELI5 — Luffy's version:**

"Ships run on **watch levels**! The deckhand steers the heading he's given — he changes NOTHING alone. The watch officer can trim sails and wake nobody. The captain changes course and everyone follows. And the fleet commander? Even the captains take his heading! Autonomy in a job is exactly that: **what may I change WITHOUT asking, and who do I have to wake if it's bigger?** If nobody ever told you your watch level, you don't have one — you have vibes."

> **⚠️ Where the analogy breaks:** a naval watch level is written and drilled; workplace autonomy is mostly *implicit* and discovered by collision — which is why the fix is to make it explicit in writing. The exact technical distinction: **autonomy is the set of decision classes you may conclude alone (tools, scope-within-agreed-bounds, technical design, customer communication) versus those requiring sign-off (budget, data access, scope change, security posture, public claims).** Tier systems are compressed descriptions of that set; two companies' "Tier 3" can differ in exactly the classes that matter.

**⚔️ STANDARD — Engineer's version:**

| Watch level (typical FDE ladder) | May decide alone | Must wake… | Evidence that earns it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **T1 — Apprentice** | Task-level implementation choices | On any scope/customer/data change | Shipped slices under review (Days 1–3 artifacts) |
| **T2 — Journeyman** | A workstream's technical approach; customer comms within brief | Scope shifts, constraint changes | A workstream delivered end-to-end with clean handover |
| **T3 — Lead** | The engagement's approach, team task split | Portfolio impacts, escalations, budget | Engagements delivered *and adopted*, matrix signed, lessons filed |
| **T4 — Fleet** | Portfolio strategy, which engagements exist | The executive committee | Multiple engagements' outcomes + the product deltas they caused |

Read it bottom-up as a *trust audit*: each level is the previous level's evidence, compounded.

**Skypiea sidebar ☠️:** the diagnostic that saves careers — when offered a "senior" title, ask for the **escalation matrix**: "which decisions still require sign-off in this seat?" If the answer is "everything except how I write the code," the title is compensation theater and the tier is T1 with a nicer hat. Conversely, junior titles with real decision rights (early-stage startups) mint senior evidence fast — the *evidence* column above is what you actually accumulate, titles just narrate it later.

#### 🔬 Reverse-Engineering Notes

Dissect the East Blue sheet's grading of the crew — **Tier 1** — against the ladder's evidence column:

1. **What the crew has:** shipped slices (v0.1.3, 14 tests), one workflow, a signed matrix — T1–T2 *engagement* evidence, but zero *multiplicity*: no second deployment, no portfolio.
2. **What the Grand Line inspector graded** ("Tier 3 paperwork") was the *governance* artifact quality — a T3-shaped *document* on a T1-shaped *wake*. Both inspectors told the truth about different columns.
3. **The transferable method:** grade yourself per-column (delivery evidence, autonomy evidence, portfolio evidence), not per-title — then the two-inspector morning stops being a contradiction and becomes a dashboard.
4. **Removal test:** delete the evidence column from the ladder and tiers become pure status — which is exactly how they're misused in the wild.

#### 🏗️ Architecture, Trade-offs & Failure Modes

- **Simplest workable approach:** write your own decision-rights table (alone / wake / never-mine) and validate it with your manager annually.
- **Alternative:** discover autonomy by collision — expensive; the wreckage is usually a customer relationship.
- **Failure modes:** *autonomy inflation* (claiming T3 decision rights on T1 evidence — symptom: escalations that arrive as incidents; fix: the evidence column before the title conversation); *autopia at T4* (nobody to wake → unreviewed strategy; fix: peer review across fleet leads — even commanders need a watch officer).

#### ⚓ The Crew Debate — "Should the crew claim T2 now?"

- **NAMI:** "The workstream evidence exists: the counter deployment, end to end." *(One customer, navigator — multiplicity is the gap.)*
- **ZORO:** "Then get the second village. Evidence, not argument." *(…Was that a plan? From Zoro? Note it in the log — problem count: 3.)*
- **JINBE (the calm):** "Claim the stripe you can defend with artifacts a stranger can verify. The sea respects nothing else."

### 🏝️ Location 3 — Separate seniority from domain specialization

#### 🎭 Journal Scene

The two inspectors, now sharing tea, compare their fleets' confusing cases. The Grand Line inspector offers one: "We have a captain — twenty years, four stripes, decorated. We sent him to the Fish-Man Island engagement. He sank it. Not for lack of seniority — he had never sailed legacy waters. The *domain* ate the *rank*."

Robin, quietly: "And we have all met the reverse: a second-year deckhand who has only ever sailed medical-data waters and outperforms admirals there."

Jinbe draws two rulers on the sand — one vertical (seniority: miles sailed, storms survived, trust earned) and one horizontal (domain: which waters — legacy integration, medical data, finance, retail, edge deployment). "Careers die," he says, "on the assumption that the vertical ruler moves you along the horizontal one. It does not. They are different rulers."

#### 📖 Feynman Notes

**🍖 ELI5 — Luffy's version:**

"Two things get confused ALL THE TIME! Your **rank insignia** — how many stripes, how long you've sailed, how much the crew trusts you — and your **chart** — WHICH WATERS you've actually sailed! Twenty years in East Blue makes you an East Blue legend. It does NOT make you a Grand Line navigator! And a Grand Blue — Grand LINE — whoever — anyway! The point: stripes say HOW MUCH experience. The chart says WHAT KIND. When someone says 'senior,' ask: senior *where*?"

> **⚠️ Where the analogy breaks:** insignia are awarded by institutions on schedules; the chart updates only when you actually enter new waters — there is no shortcut, and no institutional award for it, which is why résumés inflate the chart but interviews check it. The exact technical distinction: **seniority is cumulative general trust (scope you can own anywhere: autonomy, communication, delivery discipline); domain specialization is specific verified experience (waters you've sailed: industries, system types, compliance regimes, integration patterns).** FDE tier ladders mostly measure the first; FDE *hiring* mostly filters on the second — the mismatch is the failure mode below.

**⚔️ STANDARD — Engineer's version:**

Draw the two-axis map (seniority ↑, domain →) and place everything on it:

| | Junior | Mid | Senior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain A (your waters)** | the fast lane: seniority grows fastest where context compounds | | Domain expert: senior *here* |
| **Domain B (adjacent)** | transfer tax: 30–50% of your seniority reads as junior until you re-earn context (illustrative range, not a statistic) | | Valuable cross-pollinator |
| **Domain C (foreign)** | near-reset: trust is portable, context is not | | Senior-curious: trust opens doors, chart still empty |

Uses: choosing engagements (grow seniority in A, chart breadth in B, avoid C unless paid to learn); reading postings ("senior FDE, healthcare" = senior trust AND healthcare chart — both gates); and honesty about your own placement (your Day-2 audit is this map with evidence attached).

**Skypiea sidebar ☠️:** the compounding asymmetry. Seniority transfers at full value the day you switch; domain context compounds *only with time-in-waters* — it cannot be crammed, only sailed. Strategic consequence: domain switches are cheapest early (low seniority at stake) and most expensive at the top (you trade expert-status for senior-curious). The senior move is the *adjacent* hop — Domain B keeps most of A's value while writing the first pages of a new chart.

#### 🔬 Reverse-Engineering Notes

Dissect the inspector's failed captain against the map:

1. **Placement:** seniority senior (vertical ruler high), Fish-Man Island waters foreign (Domain C). The fleet read the vertical ruler and assumed the horizontal one — a *staffing* error, not a *personal* one.
2. **What the engagement actually taxed:** legacy-context knowledge (Day 8's island, previewed) — the exact thing rank cannot confer.
3. **The detection that was skipped:** any Domain-C placement must be priced as a learning engagement with senior Domain-A support nearby; the fleet instead billed it as an expert deployment.
4. **Removal test:** if the fleet's staffing rubric had contained the two-axis map as a required field, the misplacement is impossible to write down. Every framework today exists because its absence has a body count.

#### 🏗️ Architecture, Trade-offs & Failure Modes

- **Simplest workable approach:** the two-axis map, updated at each audit (evidence per cell, not vibes).
- **Alternative:** one ladder ("years of experience") — the industry's default, and the generator of both the failed captain and the ignored deckhand.
- **Failure modes:** *T-shaped theater* (claiming breadth nobody can verify — fix: one artifact per claimed domain, per Day 2); *domain hoarding* (refusing all adjacent hops — fix: one Domain-B engagement per year-ish, priced as learning); *seniority denial in Domain B* (expecting your A-rank to read at full value — fix: state the transfer tax out loud before accepting the seat).

#### ⚓ The Crew Debate — "Which waters does the crew sail next?"

- **FRANKY:** "Adjacent: another village's counter. Same waters, deeper chart." *(Multiplicity for the wake, yes — but breadth stays one-fleet.)*
- **ROBIN:** "A different *kind* of workflow — a clinic, a ledger office. New waters, cheap tuition while we're young." *(The syllabus's spirit. The sea agrees.)*
- **JINBE (the calm):** "Dawn Island is Domain A. The chart says: one adjacent hop when the saga's evidence closes — priced, planned, matrix-signed. Not before."
- **LUFFY:** "Can the adjacent village have MEAT?" *(…We'll check, Captain.)*

### 🏝️ Location 4 — Read hiring rubrics and delivery expectations

#### 🎭 Journal Scene

The inspectors, enjoying themselves now, stage a demonstration. The Grand Line inspector posts a hiring rubric on the harbor board — his fleet's, verbatim, anonymized: rows like "engagement ownership (30%): did the candidate define scope or receive it?" and "delivery under constraints (25%): name a deployment where access, compliance, or legacy limited you — what did you trade?"

The East Blue inspector posts hers: "miles sailed (40%): number of live deployments touched," then "incident behavior (20%)," then "customer references (15%)."

"Same title," Nami says slowly, "completely different scoring engines. Yours grades *judgment artifacts*; hers grades *survival counts*."

"Which is why," the Grand Line inspector says, "the candidates who fail are never weak. They are **strong along a ruler we weren't holding.**"

Zoro reads both rubrics twice. "So before you train for the stripe," he says, "you read the race."

"Read the race," both inspectors say, in unison, slightly annoyed to agree.

#### 📖 Feynman Notes

**🍖 ELI5 — Luffy's version:**

"Every harbor runs its OWN race! This port races around the reefs — steering wins! That port races straight lines — RAW SPEED wins! If you train steering and enter the speed race, you lose while looking great! A hiring rubric is the harbor's race rules **posted in advance** — most people never read them! They train generic 'being good at ships.' Read. The. Rules. Then train exactly THAT race."

> **⚠️ Where the analogy breaks:** race rules are fair by design; rubrics are noisy — written after the fact to justify intuitions, weighted by whatever the team last got burned by (the incident-behavior row exists because someone got burned). The exact technical distinction: **a rubric is the scoring function the panel will actually apply (weights × evidence types), and the JD is only its shadow — reconstruct the rubric by asking: what did the last person in this seat get praised and fired for? What evidence can this panel even verify? What did they just get burned by?** Delivery expectations are the rubric's other half: what "good first 90 days" means *there* — ships weekly? zero incidents? stakeholder delight? — and no two harbors weight them the same.

**⚔️ STANDARD — Engineer's version:**

The rubric reconstruction kit (run it per posting):

| Step | Question | Where the answer lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What does the panel *verify*? (repo access, references, live scenarios, case studies) | Interview process description; the questions they actually ask |
| 2 | What are the visible weights? | "Must-have" counts, panel composition (eng-heavy vs. sales-heavy), the exercise's shape |
| 3 | What recently burned them? | The one hyper-specific requirement nobody sane writes casually ("experience with on-prem air-gapped deploys") |
| 4 | What does "great first 90 days" mean there? | Ask directly; a vague answer is *itself* the finding |
| 5 | Which ruler do they hold — judgment artifacts or survival counts? | Steps 1–4 converge on it |

Then match: your evidence → their verify-list (Day 2's audit joins Day 2's JD triage — the fight column, now with the panel's scoring engine attached). The Applied Exercise tonight runs exactly this across three postings and six dimensions.

**Skypiea sidebar ☠️:** delivery expectations hide the role's real tier. "You'll own engagements end-to-end from week one" = a T3-shaped seat (and T3 chaos if unsupported). "You'll execute the lead's playbooks" = T1 execution with senior exposure — excellent mint, different race. The trap is reading *salary* where you should read *expectations*: titles and pay ride one ruler; what your Tuesday looks like rides the other.

#### 🔬 Reverse-Engineering Notes

Dissect the burned-by row ("incident behavior, 20%"):

1. **Fossil evidence:** rubric rows are fossils of incidents. A weighted row means an incident happened; its *shape* tells you which. "Incident behavior" → a past hire froze or freelanced during an outage.
2. **Your counter-move:** arrive with one incident story in STAR-with-artifacts form (what you saw, what you did *within your watch level*, what changed afterward) — you are answering the fossil directly.
3. **Weight ≠ word count:** the rubric's 20% will consume 40% of the interview if the fossil is fresh. Detect freshness by follow-up depth; answer the fossil, not the job description.
4. **Removal test:** delete step 3 from the kit and you prepare brilliant answers to questions nobody is asking — the standard outcome of generic interview prep.

#### 🏗️ Architecture, Trade-offs & Failure Modes

> **Common Failure Mode (from the syllabus, verbatim):** *"Preparing for a generic FDE title when the actual role requires a different technical profile."* **Symptom:** strong candidate, failed loop — strong along a ruler the panel wasn't holding. **Root cause:** preparing to the *title's* dictionary meaning instead of the *rubric's* scoring function. **Mitigation:** the five-step reconstruction + evidence-matching; if the rubric can't be reconstructed from public material, that fact is a finding — ask the recruiter the two questions that matter (what's verified, what's weighted). **Completion Evidence:** for your next three applications, you can state each panel's probable ruler and which artifact of yours answers it.

**Trade-offs:** rubric-tailoring costs breadth — a candidate overfit to one harbor re-runs the kit per application (it takes an hour; generic prep wastes the same hour invisibly). And some rubrics are dishonest — written for one candidate, run for another; you cannot fix that, only detect it (mismatch between stated weights and actual exercise) and price the role accordingly.

#### ⚓ The Crew Debate — "Train for the Grand Line rubric or the East Blue one?"

- **ZORO:** "Both rubrics reward one thing we control: the wake. Deploy, close, sign, repeat. Evidence answers every ruler."
- **NAMI:** "And *present* it per ruler — the same wake, framed as governance artifacts for the Grand Line fleet and deployment counts for East Blue."
- **JINBE (the calm):** "The navigator has found the practical truth: tiers are fleets' languages. Evidence is the currency; translation is the skill. The stampede after titles is just sailors refusing to learn the local language."

### 🏝️ Location 5 — Avoid treating compensation reports as stable guarantees

#### 🎭 Journal Scene

Last light. Pokkà sprints up with the fish market's chalkboard under one arm — "the merchants want to know what FDEs earn, so they can price the crew's quests!" — and a scrap of paper torn from a mainland gazette: "FORWARD DEPLOYED ENGINEERS: THE HIGHEST PAID AI ROLE" followed by a single enormous number.

Nami reads it the way she reads weather. "One number. No fleet named, no city, no seniority split, no date of collection, no sample size." She turns the chalkboard around. On it, today's fish prices — *per market stall*: "Same fish. Six stalls. Six prices. Yesterday's prices are chalked over because they're WRONG by tonight."

"The gazette's number," Jinbe says, "is yesterday's fish at one stall, published as the law of the sea."

Usopp peers at it. "So the crew is NOT rich?" (Count holds at 6 — even he didn't inflate that one.)

"The crew," Nami says, chalking their own stall, "is *evidence*. Evidence prices well in every market. Gazettes just sell ink."

#### 📖 Feynman Notes

**🍖 ELI5 — Luffy's version:**

"The fish market has a big price board! But here's the trick — it's YESTERDAY'S prices, from ONE market, and fish change price every day and every port! Compensation reports are that board! 'The average fish costs THIS!' — which fish? which port? which day? caught by whom? A price board is a **clue**, not a **contract**! Nami says: use it to know the waters, never to promise your belly a meal!"

> **⚠️ Where the analogy breaks:** fish prices at one stall are at least *real transactions*; many compensation reports mix survey self-reports (inflated), posted ranges (negotiation anchors), and recruiter estimates (marketing) into one number — worse than one stall: a blend of stalls, some imaginary. The exact technical distinction: **a compensation report is a dated, market-specific, methodology-bound statistical summary — often of heterogeneous and self-selected samples — whose useful output is a range and a trend, never a point value; it is stable exactly as long as its market conditions, which for AI roles have been shifting quarter to quarter.** Treat any single number as a claim requiring: date of collection, sample, geography, seniority split, and base-vs-total-comp disclosure.

**⚔️ STANDARD — Engineer's version:**

The five-question price-board check, before believing any comp number:

1. **When was it collected?** (AI-role comp data ages in months.)
2. **Where?** (One country? One city? Remote-band blended?)
3. **Who's in the sample?** (Surveyed self-reports? Posted ranges? Placements?) — self-reports inflate; posted ranges anchor high.
4. **What's the split?** (Base vs. equity vs. bonus; junior vs. senior — an unsplit "average" is noise wearing a suit.)
5. **What's the trend, and what's driving it?** (Demand shifts, funding cycles, tooling changes — the *derivative* is more informative than the value.)

Legitimate uses: sanity-checking an offer's order of magnitude; spotting markets where FDE demand is structurally strong; negotiating with *ranges from your own market and date*, cited as such. Illegitimate uses: picking a career on one number; anchoring a negotiation on a different market's gazette; promising yourself anything.

**Skypiea sidebar ☠️:** the advanced read is *volatility as information*. High variance across sources for the same title (the FDE case, historically) signals an immature, poorly-standardized role family — which cuts both ways: upside for evidence-rich candidates (no fixed ladder to climb, rubrics rule), risk for title-chasers (the market repriced them mid-stride). If comp reports disagree wildly, Location 4's rubric-reading skill is worth more than any salary chart — you are being paid to interpret a young market, and so is everyone hiring in it.

#### 🔬 Reverse-Engineering Notes

Dissect the gazette's number — "FDEs: the highest paid AI role":

1. **Missing fields:** date, sample, geography, split, methodology — all five check-questions fail; the number is unfalsifiable and therefore unusable except as ink.
2. **What it *does* correlate with:** typically, a small senior-heavy sample in a hot quarter — i.e., the *top of one market's range for one tier*, reported as the role's average.
3. **The correct comparison:** same market, same date, same seniority band, total-comp — usually narrowing the story from "highest paid role" to "well-paid senior band in an immature family," which is a completely different career input.
4. **Removal test on yourself:** if your interest in the field would survive the number being cut in half, proceed; if not, the gazette was the reason and the reason was a fossil fish.

#### 🏗️ Architecture, Trade-offs & Failure Modes

- **Simplest workable approach:** the five-question check + collect *your own* comparables (market, date-stamped) when it matters.
- **Alternative:** median-worship — one median for a role family spanning Location 1's entire spectrum and Location 3's entire domain map is an average of different jobs.
- **Failure modes:** *anchor capture* (negotiating from another market's gazette — fix: cite your own dated comparables); *career-by-gazette* (choosing the field for one number in an immature market — fix: the halving test); *range-blindness* (accepting "within range" without knowing the range's variance — fix: ask for the band, not the point).

**`quest_log.py` — the new pure function and command:**

```python
def summarize(quests: list[dict]) -> dict:
    """Fold a quest list into the Sunday-read numbers (Day 4).

    Kept as a pure function so the evidence is testable: same quests in,
    same numbers out -- no store, no clock, no file.
    """
    done = sum(1 for q in quests if q.get("status") == "done")
    requesters = {q.get("requester", "?") for q in quests}
    created = [q.get("created_at", "") for q in quests]
    return {
        "total": len(quests),
        "open": len(quests) - done,
        "done": done,
        "distinct_requesters": len(requesters),
        "latest_activity": max(created) if created else None,
    }


def cmd_stats(store: QuestStore) -> int:
    stats = summarize(store.list_quests())
    print(f"Quests: {stats['total']}  (open {stats['open']}, done {stats['done']})")
    print(f"Distinct requesters: {stats['distinct_requesters']}")
    print(f"Latest activity: {stats['latest_activity'] or 'no quests yet'}")
    return 0
```

### Common implementation bugs (Chopper's post-build notes)

1. **Reaching for the store's internals inside the CLI** (`store._quests`) — private state from the presentation layer; `summarize()` consumes the public `list_quests()` copies only.
2. **A hidden clock inside the fold** (calling `datetime.now()` for "latest activity") — would make the function untestable and the number unstable; activity comes from stored `created_at`/`closed_at` data only.
3. **Counting requesters with a list and `count()`** — O(n²) and duplicates double-counted; a set is the one-liner that is also correct.
4. **`max()` on an empty sequence** — the classic crash on a fresh install; guarded with the `if created else None` branch, and the empty case is pinned by its own test.

### 🔑 Answer Key (Marine Quiz)

1. Descriptions of **technical scope, customer ownership, and product influence — employer-specific**; *not* a universal credential ladder (a title is fleet-local; evidence travels).
2. Weekly output (shipped code vs. analysis) · definition of done (deployment vs. defensible conclusion) · failure shape (outage vs. wrong recommendation) · consumers (customer workflow vs. internal model/eval teams).
3. Autonomy = the set of decision classes you may conclude **alone** vs. those requiring sign-off. Made real by a written **decision-rights table** (alone / escalate / never-mine), validated with your manager — and by the escalation matrix a serious employer can show you.
4. Axes: seniority (cumulative general trust) × domain specialization (verified waters sailed). "Senior FDE, healthcare" gates on *both*: senior trust AND a healthcare chart — one without the other is the failed-captain story.
5. Diagnosis 1: **wrong-race preparation** — strong along a ruler the panels aren't holding; fix: rubric reconstruction per application, answer the fossil row. Diagnosis 2: **evidence-presentation mismatch** — the evidence exists but is framed for the wrong ruler (governance artifacts shown to survival-count panels, or vice versa); fix: same wake, translated per rubric.
6. Same market as your comparables · same collection date · sample and methodology known · comp split disclosed (base/equity/bonus) · seniority band matched. Without those, 20% of *somebody's* number is not information about *your* offer.
7. Strong set: "Which decisions does this seat conclude alone — what escalates, to whom?" · "How was the last person in this seat evaluated, and what did they get praised/fired for?" (the fossil) · "What does a great first 90 days look like — what's shipped, measured, signed?" Title and salary ride one ruler; the answers ride the other.
8. Strong plans: consolidate seniority in Domain A with one *evidence-dated* adjacent hop into B mid-year — priced as learning (transfer tax stated), matrix-signed; explicitly do **not** claim B-specialist status until one B artifact exists; refuse "senior-in-B" seats until the B chart has pages. Deliberate non-claims are the mark of a strong answer.
9. Pure because its output depends only on its input (no store, no clock, no file) — testable and stable by construction. It instantiates the day's lesson that **evidence must be checkable by a stranger** (and Day 2's decision-log discipline): a number you can't re-derive is a rumor with a decimal point.
